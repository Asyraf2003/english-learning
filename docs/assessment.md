# Assessment

## Assessment Sessions

Assessment is separate from the material session. A material session teaches and assigns work; a later assessment session checks whether the student can actually retrieve and use the material.

Do not automatically conduct a memorization/oral test immediately after teaching unless the current session is explicitly an assessment session.

## Adaptive Assessment Length

Assessment length is evidence-driven. Five or six strong questions may be sufficient when the student's mastery is already clear. Expand the assessment only when performance is uncertain or more evidence is needed.

Do not make every assessment artificially long.

## Cumulative Retrieval

Assessment is cumulative. As lessons accumulate, sample selected material from previous lessons instead of replaying every prior item mechanically.

A later lesson may contain, for example, several random questions from earlier conversations and several vocabulary items from older lessons.

Prioritize:
- recent material;
- weak or failed material;
- delayed-review material;
- occasional strong/mastered material for retention checks.

The exact mix should adapt to the student's recorded history.

## Possible Assessment Areas

Assessment may include:
- vocabulary recall;
- meaning recall;
- conversation recall;
- conversation comprehension;
- related vocabulary;
- hidden exercise vocabulary;
- classroom/instructional English;
- response to a new but related situation;
- sentence construction;
- sentence transformation;
- question formation;
- grammar patterns;
- reading;
- writing;
- speaking;
- pronunciation.

The assessment should use only the areas appropriate to the lesson and the student's current state.

## Oral Assessment

Voice sessions may assess conversation memorization, spontaneous responses, vocabulary recall, sentence production, and pronunciation.

A separate oral session may be brief when mastery is obvious, or longer when evidence is unclear.

## Mastery Gate

The teacher uses evidence to decide whether the student is ready for new material.

A meaningful failure does not erase progress, but it marks the affected item or pattern for remedial review.

Repeatedly failed vocabulary, dialogue lines, grammar patterns, or pronunciation issues should return more frequently until stable.

Do not require perfection when the available evidence is already sufficient for progression.

## Scoring

Use the smallest score that honestly represents the assessed result. Do not invent a score because a file needs to be filled.

A score such as 9/10 means exactly one assessed item was wrong or incomplete under the stated test criteria.

## Formal Exams

Formal examinations verify accumulated mastery. They should not be artificially harder than the daily training that prepared the student.

The daily cumulative workload should already require the student to retrieve old and new material under pressure. A formal exam should therefore feel familiar rather than unexpectedly difficult.

## Evidence Rule

`test-results.md` must remain empty until the corresponding assessment has actually happened and the result is known.

Never fabricate a test result, mastery status, or historical performance.
